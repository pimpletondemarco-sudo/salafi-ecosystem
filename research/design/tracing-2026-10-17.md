## 1. Common tracing styles (what the market uses)

| Feature | What the evidence shows | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|
| **Dotted letters (hollow/"dotted font")** | The most common style in cheap Amazon/Etsy alphabet books: "letters ... in their dotted form ... on multiple rows of lines"; "each letter in a dotted font". | S1, S2, S10 |
| **Dot-to-dot / dot markers** | Some books use numbered dots or colourable "dot markers" instead of a dotted letter. | S3, S4 |
| **Arrows for stroke direction** | Very common selling point: "Arrow guides to show you step-by-step how to write each letter"; "follow guided arrows"; one seller uses red arrows plus a video QR code. | S5, S6, S7 |
| **Start points + numbered arrows** | Mentioned by a kids' worksheet provider and in guides ("clear start points and numbered arrows"). | S8, S11 |
| **Grey solid model** | Not seen in Arabic listings, but some OTs and Learning Without Tears prefer a solid grey model over dots ("children don't automatically understand that the dotted lines represent a solid line"). | S12 |
| **Centreline dots vs outline** | OT pages: centreline dots match the real pen movement; a hollow outline "suits a child who needs a firm boundary". No study compares them. | S12 |
| **Guidelines** | Mostly "rows of lines" (a baseline). One Arabic practice paper uses 4 lines (ascender, midline, baseline, descender). A notes page says line spacing should be about twice the height of ب, so ascenders (ل) and descenders (ي) don't touch. Georgetown's adult book uses bound tracing paper. | S2, S13, S14, S15 |
| **Sequence on the page** | Arabic school booklets and guides use: clear model → trace dotted copies → write alone with the model visible → write with no model. "Every sheet should include a freehand practice row without dotted guides." | S11, S16, S17 |
| **Repeats per row** | Not stated in any reachable snippet. Alphabet books fill whole rows; word booklets use a few copies then a blank line. Our 1 model + 2 traces is at the low end. | S2, S16 |
| **Model on the right** | No snippet states this, but guides stress that Arabic is written right to left and children "write from right to left". It follows that the model and the start of each row sit on the right. | S9, S18 |
| **Harakat** | One guide says good sheets put tashkeel on example words; another says "small marks are hard for young eyes to distinguish" and suggests oversized harakat at first. | S10, S11 |
| **Size** | Kids' books advertise "large letters"; one pack "prints each letter much bigger, with one practice word". Exact sizes not available. | S10, S19 |
| **Price context** | Etsy Arabic tracing PDFs mostly ~$2–$6 (search snippet); our earlier scan: ~278 Hijaiyah tracing listings, avg ~$2.88 (research/market-2026-10-16.md). Goodword *Arabic Writing* print books ~$4.50. | S20, S21 |

## 2. What buyers praise or complain about

Honest answer: **no buyer review text about tracing could be reached.** What exists:

- A TpT Arabic alphabet tracing sheet with harakat: 1 review, 5/5; a top Arabic alphabet set:
  84 reviews, 4.96/5 (snippet only, reviews not readable). S10
- Seller copy says free sheets are often "too faint to follow or too dark to trace over", and one
  seller FAQ answers a "faint print" complaint. This hints that **line darkness** is a real
  complaint. S10
- Guides note harakat are **hard for young eyes to see** when small. S10
- A Muslim homeschool blog reviewer **praised the lack of faces** in a tracing board book (S22).
  This shows that parents like ours notice and value it.
- Generic guides warn that thin grey lines on cheap paper blur for beginners. S2

## 3. Which script looks like school handwriting

- **Naskh is what children learn first.** A handwriting guide (*Mastering Arabic Script*) says most
  Arab children first learn to read and write naskh, and move to ruq'a around 11–12. One weaker
  site says ruq'a from about 7. Calligraphy sites agree Naskh is the beginner script because it is
  the print script. S23, S24
- Arabic school booklets for children (كراسة تحسين الخط) are explicitly Naskh. S16, S17
- **Conclusion:** Noto Naskh Arabic is the right choice for ages 6–10. Ruq'ah would look like adult
  handwriting and drops many shapes children see in the mushaf and in books.

## 4. Our worksheet checked against the market

What the current page (Letter PNG, viewed 2026-10-17) does: English label on the far left, then
solid model word, then two dotted-outline copies to its left-to-right side; Noto Naskh 700, 62px;
one tan line under each row; harakat outlined like the letters; no start dot or arrow; no blank
"write it yourself" space.

Problems seen on the page itself:
1. **The model is on the LEFT and the copies run left-to-right.** A child writing Arabic starts
   at the right. This is the opposite of Arabic reading order.
2. **Outlined harakat and dots turn into noise.** At 62px the outlined shadda and kasra become
   tiny dashed squiggles (رَبِّي and نَبِيِّي especially) and the letter dots become little outlined
   diamonds. A child cannot "trace" a 3 mm outline of a shadda.
3. **The line is not a writing baseline.** It sits under the descender of ي (≈30px below where
   ر, ب, د and ن sit), so the letters float above the line. Children are taught to sit letters
   on the line.
4. **No independent-writing step.** Every guide and school booklet ends with writing alone.
5. **Dotted outline vs centreline:** the market's most common style is the dotted ("hollow")
   letter, so parents will recognise it. It is acceptable. A true centreline is better for pen
   movement but needs a single-stroke Naskh font with harakat, which we don't have.

## 5. Recommendations, ranked by impact

1. **Move the model to the right and run the copies leftwards** (right → left: model, trace,
   trace, blank). Put the English meaning under the model or at the right edge, not as the
   first thing on the left. *Highest impact; easy; matches how Arabic is written.*
2. **Make harakat and letter dots solid, not outlined.** Keep the dotted outline for letter
   bodies if the owner prefers it, but draw the harakat and dots as solid light grey (or a slightly
   enlarged solid mark) so they stay readable. Alternative the owner may consider: a solid light-grey
   "ghost" fill with a dotted edge — OTs favour grey models, and it keeps the harakat visible.
   This is a choice for the owner; the dotted outline is market-normal.
3. **Add one blank write-it-yourself box** after the two traces (row = model, trace, trace,
   blank). Also consider a 4th row line on the back/next page later for writing with no model.
4. **Fix the guidelines:** draw the baseline where the letter bodies sit, and a lighter dashed
   line below for descenders (ي). Optional lighter top line. Keep row height about twice the
   letter height so ي and the shadda don't hit the next row.
5. **Add a small start dot** at the right end of each traced word (one colour dot, no arrows
   per letter — these are words, not single letters, and the children are 6–10), and add
   "Start at the dot, go right to left ←" to the instruction.
6. **Size:** 62px is fine for whole words at 6–10. If space allows after adding the blank box,
   go up to ~68–72px rather than down. Keep traced copies the same size as the model.
7. **Keep:** Noto Naskh (correct school script), full harakat, white page with dark-grey dotted
   strokes (ink-friendly; darkness looks right — not too faint).
